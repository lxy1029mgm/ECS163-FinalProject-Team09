The Sixth Mass Extinction

The Silent Departure

Extinction is often reduced to a statistic line on a graph or a percentage in a report. But every data point represents a silent departure from the canvas of our world.

In 1970, the Spoon-billed Sandpiper was a familiar sight across Asia’s tidal flats. Today it is among the rarest birds on Earth. With fewer than 450 individuals believed to remain globally, this tiny migratory shorebird stands at the edge of extinction.

Recent conservation concerns is brought by the proposed G228 project of land reclamation near Beihai, one of its critical stopover habitats along the Chinese coast. For a species already struggling to survive, the loss of even a single resting and feeding ground may carry consequences far beyond what numbers can express.

This inspired a broader question: How do species disappear? Is extinction the result of a single event, or the accumulation of pressures across time, geography, and ecosystems?

It is not just the story of one bird. It is a chronicle of a planet in transition. We invite you to explore the data behind the loss from the acceleration of time to the roots of human impact.

Our Methodology

Our narrative is built upon the IUCN Red List, the world's most comprehensive inventory of the global conservation status of biological species. We filtered over 159,000 records to isolate 350+ animal species with confirmed extinction status and geographical data. This data reveals not just a loss of life, but a breakdown of an interconnected living system.

The Architecture of Threats

Explore the forces driving biodiversity loss. From habitat destruction to invasive species, each circle reveals a pathway from human pressure to biological consequence. Drill down from cause to class and ultimately to individual species.

The story of the Spoon-billed Sandpiper reminds us that extinction is rarely caused by a single dramatic event. More often, it emerges through a series of decisions about what we choose to build, protect, or overlook.

In the case of the proposed G228 project near Beihai, one frequently suggested solution has been remarkably modest: shifting the route approximately one kilometer inland to avoid critical wetland habitat used by migratory birds.

Sometimes a small change can make a significant difference. The Spoon-billed Sandpiper—and the many species that share its wetlands—are still waiting for our answer.
